Tesla is in Trouble When the Madness Dies Down

May 4, 2026

First of all, I’m 23 years old and I’m not a business analyst, I’m just an engineer who admires the core mission of Tesla. I see so much facade, gilding and mistruths floating around the high-market-cap automaker that I once respected. Don’t take any of this as financial advice, it’s a technical rant. If you’re uninterested in the shortcomings of Tesla, it’s probably best to read another article praising Elon's genius. I tried to ground this in reality as much as possible with the hyperlinks and hope you’re interested in truth. If you're an Elon hater, hate is bad, but enjoy the article.

Tesla’s valuation at this point is entirely focused on their future. It requires them to win robotics, self driving, and now chip production. As of the writing of this article, Tesla has a P/E ratio of ~340, meaning investors are paying for ~85 years of the current earnings (compare to Toyota ~10, or the S&P at ~25). The more I looked into the promises (both past and future), the more evident it became that something terrible was unfolding.

Starting on a positive note: The Tesla Megapack and Powerwall are killing it and accounted for $12.8B in revenue last year. They have excellent margins on these (which is a struggle for most automakers), and have had impressive growth YOY, but this alone is 13% of the valuation, how can that segment justify the 342 P/E). Next, there’s the Model Y, the bestselling car in 2023. And today it is still right up there in worldwide sales with 1M+ units last year, it is truly an impressive vehicle in terms of the value proposition.

My skepticism about Tesla increased dramatically in March after I read about the Terafab project. I had a knee-jerk reaction of skepticism to what I heard. The Terafab is a joint venture between SpaceX and Tesla with the goal of producing more chips than all other foundries combined.[1] They can do that with cars, right?

To paint a clear picture here, look at Tesla's claims on what they’ve announced toward that goal. The specs are: One terawatt of compute per year, 100k wafer starts per month (WSpM) and scaling to 1M (for context, TSMC’s global 3nm output is 180k WSpM), Intel’s 14A process node (comparable to 2nm), $20-25B for initial buildout. The Wikipedia page outlines this well. The plan is to have two fabs, one R&D campus near Giga Texas, and a large fab planned to handle full volume. The planned facilities will house logic and memory fabrication as well as lithography reticles (which is unheard of in the semiconductor industry).

Most importantly, though, there’s only one timeline given: when the chips will actually start fabrication. The AI5 chips will start production at the end of the year and ramp up next year, with AI6 following soon after. The catch is that they won’t be using Tesla fabs to make those. To hype the Terafab, the crucial detail of TSMC and Samsung producing those chips was conveniently left out of the presentation.

Ignoring the existing plans to utilize other foundries, is there a path for Tesla to deliver on the promises for the R&D campus? I believe they can do it someday, but I have absolutely no idea how they’re even going to have the required tools moved in by EOY. I think the biggest bottleneck worth focusing on is lithography tools, which alone will cost $6.4B[2], or ~25% of the $25B budget for the whole fab.

Now, spending 25% of your budget on lithography tools isn't that crazy since most fabs allocate ~20% of the budget to that. The problem here is that all of this was assuming 100% yield. Samsung is struggling with 2nm yields, and the best TSMC has achieved is 70%. To get 100k good wafers with that yield, Terafab would cost ~$36B.[3] To be fair, in the presentation the only metric presented around fab performance (other than compute wattage which I already explained is being outsourced) was the number of WSpM. Maybe Tesla just wants to rush it and make a bunch of broken wafers. Maybe then they could do it for $25B, but if they want to have working chips, that’s an entirely different story.[4]

I did mention at the beginning that the energy storage solutions were doing well, and they are, but the battery technology itself is not. In-house battery production accounts for less than 30% of the batteries in their cars, a goal they still haven’t hit today. The 4680 cell is widely regarded as the biggest historical failure Tesla has had. It was supposed to be in mass production by 2021, but didn’t even reach small-scale production until 2024, and to date it has only ever been used in the Cybertruck. Another failure in the energy department is Tesla’s solar program. Also on the energy front: Elon bailed bought out his cousins’ company SolarCity in 2016. The solar roof project spun off from the acquisition was such a failure that they don’t even give quotes for the solar roof anymore.

Next, let’s talk about the used Tesla market. I was car shopping in January, and looked into getting a used Model 3. I wanted one because they are cool cars, but the amount of battery degradation they have after just 5-7 years makes them unusable as anything but a city car, and it costs upwards of $8k to replace. I ended up getting a Toyota 4Runner that was three years older than the Tesla and was similarly priced. Coincidentally, weeks later, a coworker of mine sold his Model 3 that was getting <120 miles of range on a full charge. He took it on a road trip to Colorado and decided to trade it in for a 4Runner after that. If you don’t believe my anecdote though, see the statistics on the phenomenon of rapid depreciation. This makes me skeptical about how profitable robotaxi can be.

Ok, but I hear the real value is in the software. As much as fanboys like the “quirks” that have been added to the software over the years, unfortunately many average Tesla customers disagree. Because of this, Tesla is working to add CarPlay to their vehicles. This means the value is not really in the UI and OTA updates (after all, Ford and BMW do this now). It’s really about the FSD software. FSD isn’t vaporware: I’ve tried it and it’s genuinely impressive, but also will stay in beta for years to come. If you believed Elon in 2019 that it would be financially insane not to buy a Tesla; I’m sorry your car is now worth as much as a 2019 Honda Civic and still can’t self drive. The story is still that FSD is just a year away for all Teslas. Look, I live in Austin and I can tell you now it’s still going to be a while. Like Waymo, they are currently still using humans to drive the Robotaxis some of the time (At least Tesla isn’t outsourcing it to third-world countries to avoid paying minimum wage). Lastly, I ask: is there not a world where self driving software ends up getting commoditized by something like this?

As mentioned previously, the Tesla Model Y was the bestselling car in 2023. Tesla enthusiasts will claim that the Model Y has been the bestselling car in the world since 2023. Unfortunately, this is just not true. Tesla also retired the Model S and X this year. I was surprised by this, the model S is the most influential car of the 21st century, and they don’t have a replacement ready for that car (and the Model X is just gone now). The plan is to use the now-empty space for Optimus, but I think that’s nonsensical. This is like Toyota saying: “We were selling a lot more RAV 4s than NXs so we’re just gonna use the factories to build our basketball robot”.

Is there then a case for Tesla being a robotics company? I think it’s weak. Optimus today is doing the same stupid stuff that Boston Dynamic’s Atlas was doing in 2020. Humanoid robots are demo-complete at this point: if you want to make a single video to hype up the robot, you can have one with an “impressive” result. All you need is a contrived-enough environment, some good engineers, and a bit of time. I actually think Tesla’s utilization of industrial robotics would be a decent explanation for some of the valuation. However, Tesla frames Optimus as an important part of the Tesla manufacturing process. Putting humanoid robots in a factory is fundamentally the wrong approach (BMW had to learn this the hard way)[5].  A hand can never drill better than a drill; a humanoid robot can never pick and place an irregularly-shaped widget better than a KUKA arm with a KEYENCE camera and a perfectly engineered end effector. But this may go unheard in the Tesla enthusiast crowd because some believe that, despite the lack of show, they’re way ahead of everyone else and secretly have AGI humanoids.

Anyway, I hope Tesla doesn’t become the next Enron, because I’d love to see them continue making cars. Maybe there’s a path for them to just become a normal auto maker, but I’m afraid that might not be how it pans out.

  2. It is theoretically possible to mostly use Low-NA tools on a 2nm equivalent node. Let’s take the (miraculously) optimistic case and assume Tesla instantly figures out how to get the output ASML projects from the next-gen Low-NA tool on the current-gen tool. They will be able to process 220 WpH, and let’s just say they figure out how to get best-in-the-world 70% uptime from the beginning. Assuming everything else is perfect: 220 WpH × 24 hr × 30 day × 0.7 uptime ≈ 111k W/F passes per month. Then, assuming they use only 25 EUV passes: 111k W/F passes per month / 25 EUV passes = 4,435 WSpM per tool. To reach the 100k goal, they’d need 23 low-NA EUV tools. It's difficult to find reliable, current numbers on this, but each Low-NA EUV tool costs ~$200M.  It’s worth noting that Intel will also use High-NA EUV tools for 14A  not because they have to, but because it will be cheaper (if they can replace three low-NA multipatterning steps with one high-NA step it makes sense to buy a tool that’s double the price). So let’s add in one of those ($380M), and say they purchase 20 low-NA tools. Of course DUV tools will be needed for the BEOL patterning ($2B more for 20 immersion and 10 dry DUV) so now we’re sitting at $6.4B just for the lithography tools

  3. In order to hit 100k good WSpM at a 70% yield, we find that 100k WSpM / 0.7 yields . Scaling the original $25 fab by 1.43x puts the realistic cost at ~$36B. I understand that yield and fab cost do not scale linearly like that, but given all the concessions given to Tesla earlier, I feel that this figure still accurately represents the all-in costs of the $100k WSpM fab.

  4. I didn’t even address the claim that they will do 1 million WSpM because it’s so ridiculous. ASML literally is only capable of making 80 EUV tools per year for the whole world. I’m really lost on how anyone could explain to me how Tesla is going to procure the 200 EUV tools needed for the 1M wafer fab.
