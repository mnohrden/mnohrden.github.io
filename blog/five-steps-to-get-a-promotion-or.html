Five Steps to Get a Promotion, or Why Your Resume Should Never Use Times New Roman

May 11, 2026

I recently came across this article about how using Times New Roman on your resume is a bad idea. I got a paragraph or so into it when I realized how pointless it was to care about the font on my resume. At the end of the day, I’d never want to be hired because of the font on my resume. If the odds of me standing out are so low that scrutinizing the font type can make or break getting hired, there is guaranteed to be low hanging fruit that would actually make substantial impacts on the resume (i.e. the content).

If you're here looking for career advice, I have bad news. I have no advice. There is nothing I can possibly add to your particular situation when it comes to advice on how to do your job better. I could make up five steps: work harder, show up earlier, leave later, do deep work, and set SMART goals…The problem is that you already know those things would be good for you. At whatever age you're at reading this, none of these things are revelations. And they’re probably true; they’re just facts and frameworks you already know. Just reading them is a waste of your time because knowing is rarely a bottleneck to work.

But forget about my (lack of) advice, you didn’t even ask for it. Do you think anyone can give you helpful advice like that? Do you really think this guy actually figured out anything you didn't already know about power? And what am I supposed to do, memorize all 48 of them or something? Will my life be happier and better if I just memorize these things someone is telling me? The answer to all of these is almost certainly: no.

There is a genre of advice-giving all around us. Presentation, workflow, framing, and methodology are signals we optimize instead of focusing on the work itself. The illusion of competence that frameworks convey is rarely addressed because it’s pretty convincing to those watching from the outside. I’ve observed the pattern of optimizing the frame around the work at every company I've worked at, on the internet, in school, among friends, and in myself. Frameworks are learned and emerge from real work.

At my first internship, I worked on a software tool for engineers at the company. I’d only taken one class on programming before that. To learn how to code, I didn't need a coding class or some sort of meeting to learn more. I just needed a project. When I was stuck, I thought, tried, failed, tried again, and eventually created a working program. Confusion was the lesson; advice cannot produce that sort of competence. The advice comes from experience and patterns that emerge after years of struggle.

Frameworks and advice don’t just fail to produce competence, if they are the primary focus, they actively stifle it. In another internship, my department held weekly Kaizen meetings to address known, wasteful processes. The actions from these meetings mostly produced additional complexity. Outside of the scope of those meetings, I found two significant wastes just by asking why we had specific requirements for the sequence of some of the processes. Ironically, this is an underlying principle behind Kaizen. Because I was focused on the root cause of the issue, the process to solve it naturally followed a framework that has been identified before. These were two easily actionable process changes that were burning cash in the mid-five figures weekly. When I proposed changes, I was told by one of the directors: "that isn't how we do things around here". Hearing this was devastating as a college kid.

Frameworks and advice are what you end up with after doing the work properly, not what you build before it. But, for what it’s worth, I’m gonna change my resume to Helvetica.
